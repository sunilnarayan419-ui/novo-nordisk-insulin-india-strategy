# Budget Impact Analysis

## Framing
Budget impact here is assessed from two perspectives: (a) the **patient/household** budget (already covered in `06_PRICING_AFFORDABILITY/affordability-analysis.md`), and (b) the **payer/system** budget (government or insurer), which is the focus of this file.

## System-Level Budget Impact of Improved Insulin Access (Illustrative Direction, CA)
- **Near-term budget impact of expanded insulin access is a net cost increase** for any payer that finances it — more patients on appropriate therapy means more drug spend in year 1–3.
- **Medium-to-long-term budget impact is more favorable** if improved access reduces the incidence of costly complications (DKA admissions, dialysis, amputation, cardiovascular events) — consistent with the general health-economic pattern that upstream chronic-disease management reduces downstream acute-care costs, though the payback period (often 5+ years for complication-avoidance to materially offset drug spend) exceeds most single-budget-cycle planning horizons, which is a known challenge in chronic-disease health economics generally, not unique to India or insulin.

## Implication for AB PM-JAY specifically
Because AB PM-JAY finances hospitalization (where it already bears complication costs) but not routine outpatient insulin (where prevention would occur), the scheme currently has a **structural budget mismatch**: it pays for the expensive downstream event but not the cheaper upstream prevention. This is the core economic argument for a future outpatient chronic-disease benefit (see `07_MARKET_ACCESS/reimbursement-landscape.md`) — the government/NHA has a genuine fiscal incentive to consider this, independent of any manufacturer's commercial interest.

## Strategic Implication for Novo Nordisk
This budget-impact logic is the appropriate evidence base for engaging NHA/state health departments on outpatient insulin financing pilots — framed as fiscal prudence (avoided hospitalization cost) rather than only as an access/equity argument.
