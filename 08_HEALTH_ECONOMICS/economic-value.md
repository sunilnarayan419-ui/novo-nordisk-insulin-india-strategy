# Economic Value Synthesis

## Value Proposition by Stakeholder (Synthesis)
- **For government/payers**: appropriately managed insulin therapy reduces long-run hospitalization and complication costs, partially offsetting drug spend over a multi-year horizon (see `budget-impact.md`); analogue insulin's marginal advantage over human insulin is concentrated in hypoglycemia-related acute-cost avoidance for higher-risk patients specifically, not uniformly across all patients.
- **For patients/households**: appropriate therapy avoids catastrophic complication-related costs and productivity loss, but the near-term cash burden of higher-cost analogue therapy is a genuine barrier that must be addressed through pricing/access programs, not assumed away by long-run value arguments patients cannot act on given liquidity constraints.
- **For Novo Nordisk**: a credible, quantified economic-value narrative (rather than a purely clinical-superiority narrative) is the more persuasive and more honest basis for institutional/payer engagement, particularly as biosimilar competitors can make comparable clinical claims at lower price.

## Net Economic Value Estimate (Illustrative, CA)
For the subgroup of patients at meaningfully elevated hypoglycemia risk (elderly, renal impairment, historical severe hypoglycemia, Type 1 diabetes), the total-cost-of-care case for analogue over human insulin is reasonably strong even at current price differentials. For the broad, lower-risk majority of Type 2 patients, the total-cost-of-care case for analogue over well-managed human insulin is **weaker and price-dependent** — meaning blanket promotion of analogues to this majority group is not well-supported as an economic argument, even though it may be marginally supported on convenience grounds.

## Strategic Implication
This is a further argument, from the health-economics side (not just the market/access side), for the segmented portfolio strategy: **target analogue promotion economically as well as clinically to the patients who need it most**, and compete for the broader Type 2 population primarily on access/affordability/simplicity rather than a clinical-superiority claim that the total-cost-of-care evidence does not uniformly support.
