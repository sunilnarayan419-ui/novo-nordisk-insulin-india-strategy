# Price Sensitivity Analysis (Qualitative + Directional Quantitative)

## Price Elasticity by Segment (CA, directional)
| Segment | Directional Price Elasticity | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Premium private/insured | Low (relatively inelastic) | Price is not the primary constraint; clinical/convenience differentiation drives choice |
| Mainstream private (Tier 1/2) | High | A meaningful share of insulin-initiation decisions and product-switching (analogue vs. human, brand vs. biosimilar) are price-driven |
| Government/institutional | Extremely high (effectively price-is-the-decision in tenders) | Near-total price-driven procurement |
| Rural/Tier 3+ | Extremely high, compounded by liquidity constraints | Even small absolute price differences can determine whether therapy is purchased at all in a given month |

## Directional Quantitative Illustration (SA, for model purposes only — see `10_FINANCIAL_MODEL/`)
Assuming a stylized demand-response function, a **10% reduction in effective out-of-pocket price** in the mainstream private and rural segments is assumed in this report's model to be associated with roughly a **4–7% increase in treated-patient volume** in those segments (a mid-elasticity assumption informed by general pharmaceutical affordability literature in LMIC settings, not an India-insulin-specific measured elasticity, which is not publicly available at this granularity).

## Why This Matters Commercially
Because elasticity is highly segment-dependent, a single national price cut is an inefficient way to expand access — it gives up margin in the inelastic premium segment (where it isn't needed to drive volume) while potentially still being insufficient in the highly elastic rural/institutional segments. This reinforces the case for **channel-differentiated pricing** over blanket price reduction (see `pricing-options.md`).
