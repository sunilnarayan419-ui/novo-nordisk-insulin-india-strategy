# Convenience & Adherence

## Device Impact on Adherence (FACT/DBI)
- Pen devices (versus vial + syringe) are associated in the diabetes-care literature with improved dosing accuracy, reduced injection-related pain/anxiety, and modestly better adherence, particularly among elderly and visually/dexterity-impaired patients — relevant given India's aging insulin-treated population.
- Regimen simplicity (once-daily basal or twice-daily premix vs. multiple-daily-injection basal-bolus) is one of the most consistently cited adherence drivers in Indian diabetology literature, given literacy, health-system-support, and lifestyle constraints for many patients.

## Adherence Barriers Specific to India (DBI)
- Recurrent affordability shocks (inconsistent income, seasonal agricultural income for rural patients) drive intermittent rationing/skipping of doses — a distinct adherence failure mode from the device/education issues more commonly discussed in high-income-market adherence literature.
- Limited structured follow-up (few dedicated diabetes educators/nurse-led follow-up programs outside major private hospital systems) means dose-titration errors and technique problems go uncorrected for longer.

## Economic Consequence of Non-Adherence
Non-adherence and non-persistence drive poor glycemic control, which in turn drives the complication burden (retinopathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular disease, amputation) that dominates the true lifetime cost of diabetes — far exceeding the cost of insulin itself (see `08_HEALTH_ECONOMICS/budget-impact.md`).

## Strategic Implication
Investment in adherence infrastructure — patient education materials, simplified dosing tools, digital reminder/support apps, and community health worker engagement — is not a "nice to have" marketing add-on but a direct lever on the total cost of care that justifies inclusion as a core element of the recommended ecosystem strategy (see `11_STRATEGIC_OPTIONS/option-D-ecosystem-strategy.md`).
