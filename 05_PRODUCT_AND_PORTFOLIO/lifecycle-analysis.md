# Product Lifecycle Analysis

## Human Insulin — Mature / Cash-Generative, Structurally Defended by Price Control
Long past patent life, low margin, high volume, government-tender-relevant. Lifecycle strategy: defend volume and government/institutional relationships; treat as a access/relationship anchor more than a margin driver.

## First-Generation Analogues (e.g., established glargine-class, standard rapid-acting) — Late Growth / Early Maturity, Facing Biosimilar Erosion
Now subject to biosimilar competition and (for glargine) NLEM price control — margin is compressing. Lifecycle strategy: hold share through device/service differentiation while pricing is renegotiated toward a more defensible level; avoid over-investing in defending peak historical margins.

## Newer-Generation/Premium Analogues (e.g., ultra-long-acting basal, advanced combination products) — Growth Stage
Not yet subject to the same price-control or biosimilar pressure; highest current margin contribution. Lifecycle strategy: primary near-term profit engine; invest in physician education and specialist channel penetration while differentiation is freshest.

## Pipeline / Next-Generation (Global, India-Relevant Over Time)
Global innovation pipeline (e.g., further-refined basal analogues, combination insulin/GLP-1 products) will eventually reach India; lifecycle planning should assume a similar compression pattern (premium pricing window, followed by price-control/biosimilar pressure) as has occurred with glargine, and should plan the *next* wave's access/pricing strategy proactively rather than reactively (a key lesson from the glargine NLEM addition, which the industry likely under-anticipated).

## Strategic Implication
Novo Nordisk should manage its India portfolio as a **staged pipeline of margin pools**, continuously refreshing the "current premium" tier as older analogues mature into price-controlled, access-tier products — this requires proactive, not reactive, pricing/access planning ahead of anticipated NLEM expansion.
